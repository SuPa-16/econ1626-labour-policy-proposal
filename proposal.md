# AI and the Future of Work: A Policy Proposal for the Australian Labour Market

**To:** Deputy Secretary, Employment & Skills
**Department:** Employment and Workplace Relations, Australian Government
**Date:** April 2026

---

## Executive Summary

Artificial intelligence is reshaping the Australian labour market in ways that demand targeted policy action. While recent evidence suggests AI-adopting firms are creating more jobs than they eliminate, the nature of those jobs is fundamentally changing — requiring skills that current workers and education systems are not yet equipped to deliver (CSIRO, 2026). Without intervention, this skills mismatch risks deepening inequality between high-skilled, AI-literate workers and those left behind in declining task profiles. This proposal analyses AI's impacts on employment, wages, and industrial structure; evaluates three concrete policy options against clear criteria; and recommends a combined reskilling and portable entitlements package as an immediately actionable, evidence-based response.

---

## Problem Definition & Context

Artificial intelligence — encompassing machine learning, robotic process automation, and generative AI tools — is transforming how work is performed across every sector of the Australian economy. Its impact operates through three distinct channels: task automation (replacing routine cognitive and manual tasks), task augmentation (enhancing the productivity of skilled workers through AI tools), and task creation (generating demand for new AI-adjacent roles that did not previously exist).

In Australia, recent CSIRO research (2026) provides an important corrective to simplistic displacement narratives: firms adopting AI posted 36% more non-AI job advertisements than non-adopting firms over the same period, suggesting that AI is primarily redistributing rather than eliminating work in aggregate. However, this optimistic headline conceals a serious structural problem. The tasks being displaced — administrative processing, routine data entry, customer service triage, and basic financial analysis — are disproportionately concentrated among workers with lower formal qualifications, older workers, and those in regional labour markets with limited alternative employment options. Simultaneously, the roles being created demand digital fluency, AI literacy, and technical adaptability that many currently displaced workers do not possess.

The central policy problem is therefore not mass unemployment but structural skills mismatch at scale: a widening gap between what the labour market demands and what a significant share of the Australian workforce can offer. Left unaddressed, this mismatch will entrench wage inequality and reduce labour market participation among groups that are already economically vulnerable.

## Analysis of Impacts

### Employment and Task Composition

Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018) demonstrate that automation displaces workers from specific tasks within occupations before eliminating occupations entirely — a process now accelerating with the diffusion of AI into white-collar roles. In Australia, administrative, clerical, transport, and entry-level professional occupations carry the highest AI-exposure risk, as their core tasks involve information processing, routine decision-making, and document generation — functions that AI systems can now perform at significantly lower cost (CSIRO, 2023). Even highly credentialled roles such as legal research, financial modelling, and basic medical documentation are seeing significant task-level displacement as generative AI capabilities mature.

### Wages and Polarisation

AI adoption is contributing to a visible wage polarisation in the Australian labour market. Productivity gains flow disproportionately to workers who can deploy AI as a complement to deep domain expertise, widening the earnings gap between high-skill and mid-skill employees. Workers in AI-exposed roles who lack complementary digital skills face downward wage pressure as their tasks become cheaper to automate (Autor, Levy & Murnane, 2003). The result is a hollowing out of the middle of the wage distribution — fewer stable, moderate-income roles, and growing concentration at both ends of the earnings spectrum.

### Distributional and Equity Concerns

The distributional impact of this transition is deeply uneven. Workers over 50, those without post-secondary qualifications, and those in regional areas face compounding disadvantage: elevated displacement risk combined with restricted access to reskilling pathways and geographic constraints on labour mobility. For these groups, technological transition is not a neutral economic process — it actively concentrates risk among those least equipped to adapt without targeted support. Any effective policy response must foreground this equity dimension rather than treating it as secondary to aggregate employment outcomes.
