# AI and the Future of Work: A Policy Proposal for the Australian Labour Market

**To:** Deputy Secretary, Employment & Skills
**Department:** Employment and Workplace Relations, Australian Government
**Date:** April 2026

---

## Executive Summary

Artificial intelligence is reshaping the Australian labour market at a pace that existing policy frameworks are not equipped to manage. While recent CSIRO research (2026) shows that AI-adopting firms post 36% more non-AI job advertisements than non-adopters — suggesting task redistribution rather than mass displacement — the structural consequences remain serious. Finance, professional services, logistics, and healthcare are undergoing rapid task-level disruption, creating a skills mismatch that disproportionately affects lower-qualified, older, and regional workers. Without targeted intervention, this transition will deepen wage inequality and reduce labour force participation among already-vulnerable groups. This proposal evaluates three concrete policy options and recommends a combined National AI Reskilling Fund and Portable Learning Entitlements scheme, to be delivered over a phased 36-month implementation at an estimated cost of $4.0–4.5 billion.


---

## Problem Definition & Context

Artificial intelligence — encompassing machine learning, robotic process automation, and generative AI tools — is transforming how work is performed across every sector of the Australian economy. Its impact operates through three distinct channels: task automation (replacing routine cognitive and manual tasks), task augmentation (enhancing the productivity of skilled workers through AI tools), and task creation (generating demand for new AI-adjacent roles that did not previously exist).

In Australia, recent CSIRO research (2026) provides an important corrective to simplistic displacement narratives: firms adopting AI posted 36% more non-AI job advertisements than non-adopting firms, suggesting that AI is primarily redistributing rather than eliminating work in aggregate. However, this headline conceals a serious structural problem. The tasks being displaced — administrative processing, routine data entry, customer service triage, and basic financial analysis — are disproportionately concentrated among workers with lower formal qualifications, older workers, and those in regional labour markets with limited alternatives. Simultaneously, the roles being created demand digital fluency and technical adaptability that many displaced workers do not yet possess.

The central policy problem is therefore not mass unemployment but structural skills mismatch at scale: a widening gap between what the labour market demands and what a significant share of the Australian workforce can currently offer. Left unaddressed, this mismatch will entrench wage inequality and reduce labour market participation among groups that are already economically vulnerable.

## Analysis of Impacts

### Employment and Task Composition

Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018) demonstrate that automation displaces workers from specific tasks within occupations before eliminating occupations entirely — a process now accelerating with the diffusion of AI into white-collar roles. In Australia, administrative, clerical, transport, and entry-level professional occupations carry the highest AI-exposure risk, as their core tasks involve information processing, routine decision-making, and document generation that AI can now perform at significantly lower cost (CSIRO, 2023). Even highly credentialled roles — legal research, financial modelling, medical documentation — are experiencing material task-level displacement as generative AI capabilities mature.

### Industry Structure

The sectoral distribution of AI's impact is uneven but broad. In financial services and professional services, AI tools are automating compliance reporting, contract review, and basic financial analysis — compressing demand for mid-level analyst and paralegal roles. In logistics and retail, warehouse automation and AI-driven inventory management are reducing demand for routine operational workers. Healthcare is experiencing both displacement (AI-assisted diagnostics reducing administrative workload) and creation (new roles in AI oversight, data governance, and patient-facing care). The Australian Treasury (2023) identifies these structural shifts as a defining feature of the coming decade, with sectoral transitions occurring faster than workforce retraining systems are currently designed to handle.

### Wages and Polarisation

AI adoption is contributing to visible wage polarisation. Productivity gains flow disproportionately to workers who deploy AI as a complement to deep domain expertise, widening the earnings gap between high-skill and mid-skill employees. Workers in AI-exposed roles who lack complementary digital skills face downward wage pressure as their tasks become cheaper to automate (Autor, Levy & Murnane, 2003). The result is a hollowing out of the middle of the wage distribution — fewer stable, moderate-income roles and growing concentration at both ends of the earnings spectrum.

### Distributional and Equity Concerns

The distributional impact is deeply uneven. Workers over 50, those without post-secondary qualifications, and those in regional areas face compounding disadvantage: elevated displacement risk combined with restricted access to reskilling pathways and geographic constraints on labour mobility. For these groups, technological transition actively concentrates economic risk among those least positioned to adapt. Any effective policy response must foreground this equity dimension rather than treating it as secondary to aggregate employment outcomes.

## Policy Options

Three options are evaluated against four criteria: effectiveness in reducing displacement harm, cost to government, political feasibility, and equity of access.

| Policy Option | Effectiveness | Cost | Political Feasibility | Equity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Option 1:** National AI Reskilling Fund | High — directly targets displaced workers with funded training | Medium (~$2–3B / 4 years) | High — builds on existing TAFE infrastructure | High — prioritises low-income and regional workers |
| **Option 2:** Portable Learning Entitlements | High — empowers all workers to self-direct upskilling | Medium (~$1.5B / 4 years) | Medium — requires employer co-contribution mandate | High — universal access regardless of employment status |
| **Option 3:** AI Workforce Transition Levy | Medium — revenue dependent; indirect impact | Self-funding if enacted | Low — strong industry opposition expected | Medium — benefits channelled through Option 1 |

**Option 1 — National AI Reskilling Fund**
A federally funded program providing targeted training grants to workers in high-displacement sectors. Modelled on Germany's Qualifizierungschancengesetz (Qualification Opportunities Act), this fund would co-finance AI-literacy, data skills, and technical upskilling delivered through TAFE and accredited registered training organisations. Priority access would be guided by displacement risk forecasts published annually by Jobs and Skills Australia, ensuring the fund remains responsive to labour market shifts.

**Option 2 — Portable Learning Entitlements**
Modelled on Singapore's SkillsFuture programme, every Australian worker would receive an annual learning credit ($1,000–$1,500) usable across accredited upskilling providers. Employers implementing significant AI-driven workforce changes would be required to match these credits dollar-for-dollar. This option preserves individual agency, distributes adjustment costs equitably across government and firms, and creates structural incentives for continuous learning.

**Option 3 — AI Workforce Transition Levy**
A levy applied to firms whose AI adoption displaces workers beyond a defined threshold. Revenue would fund Option 1. While economically logical, this option faces significant industry opposition and complex attribution challenges, making near-term implementation politically difficult. It is best positioned as a medium-term revenue mechanism pending further consultation.

## Recommended Package & Implementation Plan

This proposal recommends implementing **Options 1 and 2 in combination**, with Option 3 deferred pending stakeholder consultation.

| Phase | Timeline | Key Actions | Lead Agency | KPI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Design | Months 1–6 | Establish cross-agency taskforce; map high-risk occupations; co-design curriculum with industry | DEWR + CSIRO | Taskforce operational by Month 3 |
| 2 — Pilot | Months 7–18 | Launch Reskilling Fund in 3 priority sectors; begin Entitlement rollout via myGov | DEWR + TAFE Australia | 10,000 workers enrolled |
| 3 — Scale | Months 19–36 | National rollout; activate employer co-contribution; annual curriculum review | DEWR + ATO | 100,000 workers trained; 60% in-employment rate 6 months post-training |

Estimated total cost: **$4.0–4.5 billion over four years**, partially offset by reduced income support expenditure and increased tax revenue from re-employed workers. This is comparable in scale to the Government's JobTrainer investment (2020–2022) and is a proportionate response to a structural disruption of equivalent magnitude.

## Risks & Mitigations

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low worker uptake due to access or awareness barriers | Medium | High | Simplified myGov enrolment; proactive outreach via Centrelink, unions, and community organisations |
| Training curricula becoming outdated as AI capabilities advance | High | Medium | Annual review cycle aligned to Jobs and Skills Australia forecasts and industry input |
| Employer non-compliance with co-contribution obligations | Medium | Medium | ATO enforcement mechanisms; mandatory public reporting for large employers |
| Political resistance from industry and fiscal conservatives | Medium | Low | Frame as national competitiveness investment; cite Germany and Singapore as precedents |
